# On Trying Comedy

In 2023, every day for a year, I did open-mic stand-up comedy. I find that some people can have the tedency as adults to reconstruct the lives they had as a kid. I remember as a kid dinnertime was full of fun and laughter, so I think part of my motivation to do stand-up was to go where there was fun and laughter around dinnertime. I didn't become a professional comedian. But I did learn a thing or two.

Comedy was a lot of trial and error. I found that what separated good comedians from bad comedians is  good comedians would constantly try new jokes, where bad comedians would tell the same jokes over and over again. My god. It was like, we've heard this one three hundred times already. The great ones, they weren't afraid to fail. They weren't at the open mic to look cool or seem like a funny person. To them, it was a learning opportunity. They'd tweak and tweak and change and shuffle and tweak the same idea for a joke, until the joke got the laughs they wanted and met their bar of quality, they'd add it to their set of "A" material, take it to a weekend show, and they'd let it rip there and bring the house down. That was so cool to watch.

I got practice in public speaking. It helped me to learn how to be myself on stage. Or rather, to learn that being myself is *totally enough* on stage. I think a lot of my early mistakes in comedy were the trying to be someone who I wasn’t, trying to be someone who I thought the crowd would like. It turned out, being me was just fine.

Through repetition, the crowd also helped me to figure out who I am. That’s the beautiful thing about comedy. I think a stand-up show is really a collaborative exercise between the crowd and the comedian, where the comedian is making the crowd laugh, and by laughing, the crowd is telling the comedian which parts of their act are truly authentic. Those were the jokes that got the best laughs. The jokes that only *that* comedian could make, because they were true and unique to *that* comedian.

For example, I learned through comedy that my family is important to me. That's really the topic that my jokes centered around. One thing unique about my family is that my parents met when they were three years old (their parents were friends). And this discovery led to a bit where I conjectured what it would be like if they actually dated at three. (*Dad: "I like the way ya crawl"* *Mom: (spits up)... "Sorry, I'm nervous"* *Dad: "Don't worry, I got a towel back at my crib"*). I had knee slappers like that. A far cry from the macho man that I was playing at the beginning, who was telling jokes that I won't repeat.

Comedy helped me to develop this style of writing, as I was writing for the self I was becoming on stage. I take pride having a consistent style of writing that is my own. In a world with piles and piles of soulless and bland AI-generated text, I think that human writing and voice matter that much more. I have read so much AI text at work that I know when it is AI. I appreciate it when I see human writing, with all its quirks and mistakes, because I think to myself that whoever wrote that cares. They care enough to write it out. That’s what I want someone to think when they see my writing, that I care.

Of course that’s not to say that everything should still be written by hand. There’s a time and a place for AI-writing: rote instructions, diagrams, communication with someone across the company you don't know that well, filling in forms. AI english is great for all of that. It checks the box.

But to bring it back to comedy, I quit doing it because I felt that I would be happier not doing it anymore. It was a total grind, late nights, listening to a ton of bad jokes, weathering the sets that didn’t go well. It felt relieved, honestly, when I quit. But I did so knowing that I had tried something totally uncomfortable, and stuck with it for some time. And I could take those learnings and fold them into what’s next.
