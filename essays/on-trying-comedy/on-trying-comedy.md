# On Trying Comedy

In 2023, every day for a year, I did open-mic stand-up comedy. It was a humbling time. But it taught me a few things.

I learned that creating good content requires discipline. The comedians I met that I respected weren’t all outrageously funny people all the time. In fact, I was surprised at how many of them *weren’t* necessarily funny people. But the good ones were extraordinarily disciplined. Constantly writing new jokes, evolving their act, doing the painful exercise of showing up to open mics to hone new material in front of other comedians. Funny took intention and effort.

I got practice in public speaking. It helped me to learn how to be myself on stage. Or rather, to learn that being myself is *totally enough* on stage. I think a lot of my early mistakes in comedy were around trying to be someone who I wasn’t, someone who I thought the crowd would like. It turned out, being me was just fine.

Through repetition, the crowd also helped me to figure out who I am. That’s the beautiful thing about comedy. I think a stand-up show is really a collaborative exercise between the crowd and the comedian, where the comedian is making the crowd laugh, and by laughing, the crowd is telling the comedian which parts of their act are truly authentic. Those were the jokes that got the best laughs. The jokes that only *that* comedian could make, because they were true and unique to *that* comedian.

For example, I learned through comedy that family is important to me. That's really the topic that my jokes centered around. Like how my mom and dad met with they were three years old, and what would it be like if they were dating at three years old? (*Dad: "I like the way ya crawl"* *Mom: (spits up)... "Sorry, I'm nervous"* *Dad: "Don't worry, I got a towel back at my crib"*). Cute stuff like that. A far cry from the macho man that I was playing at the beginning, who was telling jokes that I won't repeat.

Comedy helped me to develop this style of writing, as I was writing for the self I was becoming on stage. I take pride having a consistent style of writing that is my own. In a world with piles and piles of soulless and bland AI-generated text, I think that human writing and voice matter that much more. I have read so much AI text at work that I know when it is AI. I appreciate it when I see human writing, with all its quirks and mistakes, because I think to myself that whoever wrote that cares. They care enough to write it out. That’s what I want someone to think when they see my writing, that I care. Because I do.

Of course that’s not to say that everything should still be written by hand. There’s a time and a place for AI-writing, rote instructions, diagrams, communication with someone across the company you don't know that well, filling in forms. AI is great for all of that.

But to bring it back to comedy, I quit doing it because I felt that I would be happier not doing it anymore. It was a total grind, late nights, listening to a ton of bad jokes, weathering the sets that didn’t go well. It felt relieved, honestly, when I quit. But I did so knowing that I had tried something totally uncomfortable, and stuck with it for some time. And I could take those learnings and fold them into what’s next.
